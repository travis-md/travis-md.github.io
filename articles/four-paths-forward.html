Four Paths Forward for Support Teams in the Age of AI

April 2026 7 min read

The question isn't whether AI changes support organizations. It already has. The question is which direction you want to move — and whether you're making that choice deliberately or having it made for you.

I spent four years running enterprise customer support at AWS: 5,000-plus enterprise customers, five global regions, a $500M operation. In that time I built and deployed GenAI tools across the org, rebuilt team performance from the ground up, and developed models for how support actually functions at scale. What I've seen — and what I'll lay out here — is that there are four real strategic paths available to support organizations right now. They're not hypothetical. They're being executed today, by teams that chose to move.

Here's what each one looks like in practice.

Path 1: Augmentation

AI handles commodity work. Humans handle judgment work. The organization redesigns itself around that distinction.

This sounds obvious. It isn't. Most teams that claim to be doing augmentation are actually just bolting AI onto existing workflows and hoping for efficiency gains. Real augmentation requires a clear-eyed capability map: what does this AI actually do well, what does it do poorly, and how do we build the org around that reality?

At AWS, we built a GenAI adoption program grounded in exactly that kind of mapping. We identified the high-volume, pattern-driven work — ticket summarization, response drafting, knowledge retrieval — and built tooling around those use cases. Then we protected the judgment-intensive work: de-escalation, ambiguous technical diagnosis, relationship-sensitive conversations. We didn't ask humans to compete with AI at AI's strengths. We asked them to do what AI can't.

The result: 93% adoption across the team, 45% capacity increase, no headcount growth.

What this path requires: strong AI governance (you need to know when the AI is wrong), clear measurement of outcomes rather than throughput, and leadership that can articulate to the team why this makes their work better rather than more precarious. If you can't answer that last question honestly, you're going to have an adoption problem regardless of how good the tooling is.

Path 2: Tiered Intelligence

This is a structural play. Instead of the legacy Tier 1 / Tier 2 / Tier 3 model built around case complexity, you rebuild the tier structure around AI capability.

Tier 1 becomes AI-first with human escalation. Most contacts are handled without a human in the loop. The humans in this tier are monitors and escalation specialists, not front-line responders.

Tier 2 becomes the judgment tier. These are your more experienced people, handling cases that require real diagnosis, customer empathy, or contextual reasoning AI can't replicate. This tier is harder to staff, better compensated, and requires deeper ongoing training.

Tier 3 becomes strategic relationship management — proactive, high-touch, focused on outcomes rather than cases. This is where you put your strongest operators.

This is not a headcount reduction strategy. It is a headcount reorientation strategy. The people who were doing Tier 1 commodity work either move up or out — and the organization has to be honest about which. If you're not willing to invest in developing Tier 1 talent into Tier 2 capability, this path won't work.

What makes it work structurally is the escalation infrastructure underneath. When I built out sub-24-hour SLA coverage with standardized triage protocols across 24/7 cross-regional support, that infrastructure is what allowed Tier 1 AI to function — because there was a reliable, well-designed handoff when the AI hit its limit. Without that, the AI-first tier just becomes a frustrating delay.

Path 3: Customer Intelligence Function

Here's the path that most support leaders underestimate and most executives don't see coming.

Support organizations have the most current, highest-fidelity picture of customer experience in the company. More current than NPS surveys. More specific than QBRs. More granular than sales feedback. When AI is handling the volume, the humans in your org are free to do something more valuable: synthesize what they're hearing into signal that the rest of the company can act on.

This repositions support from a cost center that responds to problems into an intelligence function that surfaces them before they compound. That's a fundamentally different value proposition — and it earns support leaders a seat at the product and strategy table.

I built this out through two mechanisms. The first was my MCX model: 192 strategic accounts, 62 dedicated resources, structured to generate qualitative insight from our highest-value relationships. The second was sprint-based reporting across all five global regions — a cadence that forced pattern recognition and created a consistent feedback loop from frontline to leadership.

What this requires: investment in the humans who can analyze and communicate patterns, not just resolve tickets. Feedback infrastructure that connects support data to product roadmaps. And executives who are willing to actually use what support surfaces.

Path 4: AI Training Ground

This one is structural in a different way — and it's where forward-thinking support orgs are building durable organizational advantage.

Support organizations are uniquely positioned to build, test, and refine AI tools. They have the data. They have the use cases. They have the real-time feedback loops. When the team that trains the AI tools also owns the prompt library, evaluates new tooling, and exports AI knowledge to the broader organization, support becomes a center of AI capability — not just a consumer of it.

We built PromptVault for exactly this reason. It wasn't a top-down initiative — it was built collaboratively with the frontline team, who understood the edge cases and failure modes better than anyone above them. That library became a living institutional resource. And the team that built it developed a kind of AI fluency that made them genuinely useful to adjacent functions trying to figure out how to apply these tools.

What this requires: leadership that treats frontline insight as a strategic asset. A culture where the people closest to customers have a real voice in tool design. And the organizational patience to invest in capability-building that doesn't show up immediately in handle time.

These Paths Are Not Mutually Exclusive

The strongest support organizations will pursue all four simultaneously. Augmentation creates the capacity. Tiered intelligence creates the structure. Customer intelligence creates the strategic value. AI training creates the organizational capability.

They reinforce each other. The capacity freed by augmentation is what funds the investment in intelligence and training. The insights generated by the customer intelligence function inform what AI tools get built in the training ground. The tier structure creates the clear delineation between human and AI that makes all of it coherent.

The leaders who see this clearly now — who are building toward all four rather than picking one and hoping for the best — have a head start that will be very hard to close. The window for deliberate strategy is open. Use it.

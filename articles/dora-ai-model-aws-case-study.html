How I Applied the DORA AI Capabilities Model to a $500M Support Org — Before It Existed

April 2026 7 min read

When DORA published their 2025 AI Capabilities Model, I read through it and had one of those unsettling moments of recognition. Not because I'd been following their research. Because I'd been living it — for four years, at AWS Enterprise Support, without ever having the vocabulary they were about to give me.

Here's what that actually looked like.

The Org I Inherited

In 2021, I took over a struggling support organization at AWS. The numbers were blunt: 51% of my team was meeting performance expectations, job satisfaction sat at 67%, and CSAT for our most critical accounts had bottomed out at 15%. Fifteen percent. These were enterprise customers spending tens of millions of dollars a year, and we were failing them at an alarming rate.

I didn't have a framework. I had a mess and a mandate.

What I did have was a conviction that no technology intervention — no tool, no platform, no AI — was going to fix an organization that didn't trust itself, didn't know what it was doing, or didn't care about the outcomes. So I started there.

Capability 1: User-Centric Focus — Building a Team That Actually Cared

The first thing I did was boring. I rebuilt the team from the inside out. ICF coaching frameworks. Structured 1:1s with real developmental substance. Performance frameworks that gave people clarity on what "good" looked like and a path to get there.

This wasn't an AI initiative. It wasn't even an operations initiative. It was a people initiative. But DORA's research is emphatic that user-centric focus — genuinely orienting your organization around the people it serves — is a prerequisite for everything else. You can't automate care. You have to install it first.

Eighteen months later: 89% of the team exceeding expectations. Job satisfaction at 100%. A 40% internal promotion rate. The team didn't just perform better — they wanted to.

Capability 2: Working in Small Batches — Rebuilding How We Responded to Crisis

With the team stabilizing, I turned to the operational wreckage. A 15% CSAT score doesn't happen because of one bad process. It happens because everything is broken at once.

My approach was deliberately narrow. I built an escalation framework with a hard sub-24-hour response SLA. Standardized triage teams. A 24/7 cross-regional coverage model so the baton actually got passed instead of dropped.

None of this was elegant. It was structured, repeatable, and — critically — reversible. When something wasn't working, we could see it and adjust. That's what DORA means by small batches and version control: not just software deployments, but any process that can be iterated and rolled back when it fails. Within four months, CSAT went from 15% to over 90%.

Capability 3: Quality Internal Platform — Building Infrastructure That Scales

Fixing the crisis bought time. What I built next was meant to prevent the next one.

The Marquee Customer Experience model — MCX — was a dedicated framework for our 192 most strategic accounts: 62 dedicated resources, sub-one-hour SLA response, structured health monitoring cadences, risk classification frameworks, proactive outreach protocols. Not reactive firefighting. Actual infrastructure.

This is what DORA calls a quality internal platform: the scaffolding that lets your people do their best work without reinventing the wheel on every case. The platform doesn't replace judgment — it creates the conditions where judgment can be applied consistently at scale.

Capability 4: Healthy Data Ecosystems — Cleaning the Pipes

Here's the one nobody talks about, and the one that matters most for AI.

I led a program called B.E2E — a full redesign of our billing support end-to-end. We reduced TAM and support touches by 50%. Eliminated unnecessary handoffs. Built CUDOS logic for automated EDP verification.

The point wasn't efficiency for its own sake. The point was that billing data — one of the most critical signals in enterprise support — was a swamp. Ambiguous, duplicated, manually touched at every step. No AI system was going to make sense of it until we made sense of it first.

DORA's research is direct on this: healthy data ecosystems and AI-accessible internal data are foundational capabilities. You can have the most sophisticated model in the world deployed against broken or inaccessible data and get nothing useful out of it. We cleaned the pipes before we turned on the water.

Capability 5: Measurement and Feedback Loops — The Sprint Reporting System

Around the same time, I built a sprint-based reporting system covering account health, team KPIs, and segment performance across five regions. 99.9% SLA adherence. 98.5% CSAT.

What I was really building was a feedback loop. Small, regular cycles of measurement and adjustment. Not a quarterly business review that told you what happened three months ago. A weekly cadence that told you what was happening now and let you do something about it.

This is version control for operations. You need to know where you've been to know if you're improving. And when you deploy AI into this kind of environment, you can actually tell whether it's helping.

Capability 6: The Payoff — GenAI That Actually Stuck

In 2024, we deployed the GenAI program.

Amazon Q, Genie, the Support Ops Assistant, SageMaker integrations, Bedrock workflows. A comprehensive AI training curriculum with certification pathways. A PromptVault prompt library built collaboratively by the team.

The adoption rate was 93%. Production capacity increased 45% without adding headcount.

I want to be honest about why that happened — because it wasn't the tools.

We had already built a team that trusted leadership and believed in their own development. We had clean, structured processes they could describe clearly enough to automate. We had a platform that gave them consistent infrastructure to work from. We had clean data pipelines feeding the right inputs into the right systems. And we had measurement systems that could tell us, in real time, whether any of this was working.

The AI landed in fertile ground because we had spent four years preparing the soil.

What DORA's Model Validated

Reading DORA's 2025 AI Capabilities framework, I wasn't surprised by the research. I was surprised by how precisely it mapped to the sequence I'd stumbled into.

The seven capabilities they identify aren't a checklist you work through once. They're a system. Miss one and the others underperform. Build them in rough sequence and they compound. DORA's data shows organizations with all seven capabilities are significantly more likely to see real performance gains from AI. I believe it. I watched it happen.

What I didn't know — and what I wish I had known earlier — is that this is the blueprint. There isn't a shortcut to 93% AI adoption. You earn it.

What Support Leaders Should Take From This

You don't have to do this in the exact order I did. My order was partly strategic and partly just the sequence in which problems became urgent.

But you do have to do it.

If your team doesn't trust leadership, start there. If your processes are undocumented and inconsistent, start there. If your data is a mess, start there. If you don't have meaningful feedback loops, start there.

The AI will still be there when you're ready. And when you are ready — actually ready — it will perform in ways that feel almost unfair compared to organizations that skipped the foundations and went straight for the tools.

Find your biggest gap. Start closing it. The rest follows.

Morgan Travis is an operations and AI transformation leader who spent four years building what DORA now calls an AI-ready organization — at AWS Enterprise Support.
